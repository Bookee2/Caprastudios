I audited the live site against what designers now recognise as “made with AI”, and researched what studios are doing to stand apart. This page is built in the direction I recommend, so you can judge a light background by looking at it. Press the switch in the top bar to see the same page under black light.

Would white do better?

Not on its own. Off-white with one accent is now the second AI default. Go light where people read, stay dark where things glow, and fill whole sections with flat colour.

More colours?

Yes, and you already own them. The pond’s four light palettes give eight paint colours. Use them as big flat fields. Drop the cyan-violet-pink gradient.

Fonts and logo?

Keep both. Red Hat is not one of the fonts that reads as AI. Add one more voice: your own hand, from the graffiti tag.

What reads as AI on the site today

Designers now describe two generations of AI look. The first is the purple-gradient, dark-mode, rounded-card page. The second is the “tasteful fix”: small all-caps labels, 01 / 02 / 03 numbering, one accented word in the headline, fake window dots, hairline rules. The site has a full set of both. Some of this is my doing. The gradient line, the spotlight borders on cards and the rising headlines all arrived in the refresh I built this week. Spotlight borders in particular are a staple of the component libraries that AI tools reach for. They made the site more polished and more generic at the same time.

What already does not look like AI. Keep all of it.

  • The ribbon unicorn logo.
  • The koi pond, the graffiti tag and the four light palettes.
  • Red Hat type. The fonts that read as AI are Inter, Geist, Space Grotesk and Instrument Serif.
  • Real product screenshots, with no stock photos.
  • The Blender pieces: the turntable, the shards, the goat film.
  • A left-aligned, asymmetric hero. The AI default is centred.
  • Text contrast passes (about 7:1), and motion respects reduced-motion settings.

What the research says

Why AI sites look alike

A model with no brief picks the most common answer. For web pages that is Inter, a blue-to-purple gradient, three rounded cards in a row and dark mode nobody asked for. One vendor report puts that gradient on roughly three quarters of new marketing pages; treat the number as directional, but the pattern is agreed across every source I read.

The fix became a tell too

When people tell an AI to “make it tasteful”, it reaches for a second set of defaults: cream with a terracotta accent, near-black with one acid colour, serif italics on one word, mono labels, numbered sections. One open-source scanner now lists 67 tells across both sets. So swapping your navy for off-white, and changing nothing else, moves you from the first set to the second.

What people are doing instead

  • Visible human hands. Hand-drawn arrows, rough underlines, custom lettering, slight misalignment. Perfect now reads as fake.
  • Texture and mixed media. Paper, print grain, halftone, collage. Combining techniques in one image is something no single AI tool does by default.
  • Colour in blocks. Whole sections in one flat colour with clear text, as Oatly does, on a base that stays about 60% neutral.
  • Real people and places. Actual photos and a founder’s voice in place of stock and slogans.
  • One directed motion idea. An Awwwards juror’s phrase for the winners: “the motion has a director.” You already have this.

Most of these sources are agency blogs and developer write-ups, so I weighted the points they agree on and ignored the ones only one of them makes.

The white background question

The site’s character comes from light in the dark: koi that glow, paint that fluoresces. A fully white site would switch that off. A fully dark site keeps hiding it inside the default AI colour scheme.

So do both, on purpose. Think of it as one wall seen under two lights.

  • Daylight for reading: work descriptions, process, AI consulting, questions, contact. A chalky wall white, with full-colour paint on it.
  • Dark rooms for anything that glows: the pond, the films, the live GPU pieces.
  • Colour fields between them: each project and page gets one paint colour as its whole background.

The wall white should be a chalk or primer tone like this page, not a cream. Cream plus one warm accent is the look to avoid; chalk plus eight saturated paints is nowhere near it.

A stronger version: make the switch part of the brand. A lamp in the navigation turns the whole site from daylight to black light, and the paint fluoresces the way the hero’s tag does. It is a dark mode nobody else has a reason to build. Try it on this page with the button in the top bar.

Rules that keep eight colours from turning into noise

  • Flat fields only. Gradients live inside artwork (the ribbon render, the pond) and nowhere in the interface.
  • One colour per section, and one colour per page that it owns: TrailGoat is lagoon, Purple Squirrel is violet, the studio is orange.
  • Text is ink or chalk, never a colour on a colour.
  • Over a full page, chalk and ink still cover more than half of the screen.
  • The logo stays ribbon blue everywhere.

What changed from today: no labels above headings, no numbering, no rounded panels, no gradient. Sections are hard-edged colour fields. Pictures sit like paste-ups, slightly off-square, with tape and a sticker. A handwritten note points at the thing worth touching. The copy is first person and says what each thing does. Type and voice

Type

Keep Red Hat Display, Text and the logo lettering. Two changes:

  • Retire the mono all-caps labels. Where a label is needed, use plain Red Hat Text in sentence case.
  • Add a hand layer for six to ten moments across the site: an arrow, an underline, a circled word, a signature. The best source is your own marker on paper, photographed. The graffiti font already loaded for the hero tag can stand in until then, as it does on this page.

If you want to be persuaded further: Red Hat Display at 900 is doing a lot of work alone. A second display face for one-off words (a stencil, or a heavy condensed poster face) would add range. I would wait and see how the hand layer feels first.

Voice

The headlines are polished and say very little about you. Slogans in matched pairs are what AI writes by default. First person and plain facts are what it rarely writes.

Keep “Imagination. Made tangible.” as the one brand line. Rewrite the rest so each says what the thing is. The best method is to talk it through out loud and keep your own phrasing; my drafts below are still AI drafts.

Three sizes of change

A. Strip the tells

Small. Stays dark.

  • Remove labels, numbering, the gradient, panel borders and glows.
  • Flat pond colours as accents.
  • Plain first-person headlines.
  • Your photo and a few hand marks.
my pick

B. Paint the walls

Medium. Daylight plus dark rooms.

  • Everything in A.
  • Chalk reading sections, colour-field sections, dark stages.
  • A colour for each project and page.
  • Paste-up pictures with the halftone treatment.

C. Lights on, lights off

Large. Two full modes.

  • Everything in B.
  • A lamp in the navigation that turns the whole site to black light.
  • Twice the screens to design and test.

I recommend B, built so that C can be added afterwards: if the colours are set up the way they are on this page, the black light mode is a second set of values, not a rebuild. The hero would need one decision: whether the pond sits on a chalk page as a dark picture, as in the sketch, or keeps a dark top section.

What I would need from you

  • Which size: A, B or C.
  • A real photo or two: you, your desk, somewhere in Atlanta.
  • About ten marks in your own hand with a fat marker on white paper: arrows, underlines, a circle, “Kris”, “new”. A phone photo is enough.
  • Five minutes of you describing each project out loud, for the copy.
  • Whether “Imagination. Made tangible.” stays as the opening line.
